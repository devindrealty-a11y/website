What Budget 2026's housing changes mean in Greater Vancouver

Ottawa's Budget 2026 proposes raising the optional mortgage insurance limit to $1.5 million, allowing 30-year amortizations for some buyers, and extending the foreign buyer ban on existing homes to 2029 while exempting new homes. Here's what that could mean in Greater Vancouver and the Tri-Cities, BC.

By Devin Desaulniers, Real Estate Associate, Axford Real EstatePublished November 8, 2026 · Greater Vancouver & Tri-Cities, BC

Ottawa released Budget 2026 on November 5. Two proposals matter most if you're buying or selling in Greater Vancouver or the Tri-Cities, BC: a higher price limit for optional mortgage insurance, and a change to the foreign buyer ban that would exempt new homes. Greater Vancouver REALTORS® walked through the details on November 6. These are still proposals, not rules you can use at the bank tomorrow — but they're worth understanding now.

More room on insured mortgages

Budget 2026 proposes raising the home price limit for optional mortgage insurance from $1 million to $1.5 million. For those insured mortgages, it would also allow 30-year amortizations when at least one borrower is a first-time homebuyer or the home is newly built. The government says the changes are meant to encourage more competition between lenders and give buyers more flexibility.

That $1 million cap has been a real pinch locally. In October, the MLS® benchmark price for a Metro Vancouver townhouse was $1,006,500, according to Greater Vancouver REALTORS® — right around the current limit. That's familiar territory in the Tri-Cities, BC, where a lot of the buyers I work with are looking at townhomes and smaller homes in that range. If the new ceiling takes effect, more of those purchases could qualify for insured financing, which can open up more lender options.

A longer amortization can lower the monthly payment, which is the part most first-time buyers feel first. The trade-off, as the BC Real Estate Association notes, is that you may pay more interest over the life of the mortgage. GVR also points out that the government has not announced an implementation date for these mortgage changes, so nothing changes on your next pre-approval until the rules are actually in force.

The budget also sets February 1, 2027 as the start date for new mortgage-insurance rules aimed at "missing middle" housing, such as three- and four-unit homes. That's more of a builder story, but it could add housing choices over time if projects are easier to finance.

The foreign buyer ban, with a new-home exemption

The other headline is the foreign buyer ban. The federal government proposes extending the ban on foreign purchases of existing homes by two years, to January 1, 2029, while exempting new residential properties. The current ban is set to expire on January 1, 2027. Ottawa says the goal is to keep most of the existing-home market available to Canadian buyers, while directing foreign investment toward new construction that adds supply.

GVR's chief economist Andrew Lis welcomed that tweak, saying it "will allow investment in new development and should help support the construction of new homes." The Canadian Real Estate Association and the BC Real Estate Association had advocated for this kind of change.

Here's the local catch. B.C. still charges an additional property transfer tax on foreign buyers. BCREA's read is that unless B.C. adjusts its own rules, "the federal exemption may have limited practical effect in the province." Tom Davidoff, an associate professor at UBC's Centre for Urban Economics and Housing, put it more bluntly to CHEK News: he thinks it will have "approximately no effect in British Columbia because foreign buyers already face a 20 per cent tax which is pretty prohibitive." The details of who would qualify for the new-home exemption, and when it would start, are still to come.

What this means for you

If you're buying: Don't rebuild your budget around rules that aren't in force yet. If you're a first-time buyer, or you're looking at a new home, the proposed 30-year amortization and the higher insurance ceiling could help once they land — especially in that $1 million to $1.5 million band that's common across the Tri-Cities, BC. Until then, shop with today's mortgage rules and a current pre-approval. My buyer resources page has a mortgage calculator and the steps I walk buyers through.

If you're selling: I wouldn't price a resale home as if a wave of foreign demand is about to arrive. Local experts don't expect the exemption to change much here while the provincial tax stays in place. New construction could see a bit more interest over time if federal financing starts to favour it.

If you're a newcomer: These proposals don't change how you rent or buy this month. If you're settling in Greater Vancouver first and buying later, that's a common path. My guide to relocating to Greater Vancouver covers neighbourhoods across the Tri-Cities, BC and beyond.

Looking for a realtor in Coquitlam or Port Moody? I'm happy to walk through how these changes might apply once more details are published.
