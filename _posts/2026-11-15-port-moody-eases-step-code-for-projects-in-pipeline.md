Port Moody council voted this week to give about 2,474 homes in 20 development projects a break from the city's next round of stricter energy rules for new buildings. It's a fairly technical bylaw change, but it touches the cost of new condos and townhomes in one of the Tri-Cities, BC's busiest building areas, so I want to explain it in plain language.

## What council actually decided

Port Moody uses two provincial tools to set energy rules for new construction: the BC Energy Step Code (how efficient a building is, think insulation, windows and airtightness) and the Zero Carbon Step Code (how much greenhouse gas it gives off). The city was an early adopter in 2019 and later sped up its timeline, so the next step up was set to kick in on January 1, 2027, ahead of the province's own targets.

At the November 10 meeting, according to [The Inlet Current](https://inletcurrent.ca/city-council-meeting/2026/11/13/step-code-amendments/), council unanimously gave three readings to a bylaw amendment that offers "in-stream protection" to projects already well along in the approval process:

- Smaller multi-unit projects (four or more homes, three storeys or less) that applied before January 1, 2026 could be built to Step 4 instead of Step 5. Staff said that covers 10 projects with 48 homes.
- Larger projects that reach first reading by March 8, 2027 could be built to Step 3 instead of Step 4. That covers 10 projects with 2,426 homes.
- In both cases, permits must be approved and complete building permit applications submitted by December 31, 2027.

The [City of Port Moody's council highlights](https://www.portmoody.ca/city-government/council/council-meetings/council-highlights/) say the goal is to give "greater certainty and flexibility for developers while having a minimal impact on the City's climate action goals." Final adoption is expected at the November 24 meeting, and council also asked staff to come back before year-end on the city's step code deadlines, given possible provincial changes to the step code and building code.

## The cost-versus-climate debate

This wasn't a slam dunk. Coun. Callan Morrison, using figures from the staff report, said the jump from Step 4 to Step 5 could add nearly $28,000 to a $900,000 condo, against estimated energy savings of about $108 a year per household. Mayor Haven Lurbiecki and Coun. Amy Lubik raised concerns about higher emissions and about shifting energy costs onto the people who eventually live in these homes. Staff estimated the change would add roughly 35 to 39 tonnes of carbon dioxide a year across the community.

The development industry wants more. The [Urban Development Institute](https://udi.org/knowledge/research/library/port-moody-esc-zcsc-deferral) supported the city's plan but said it doesn't "go far enough to address the underlying cost challenges," and called for a further deferral until the province gives clear direction.

## What this means for you

**If you're looking at a presale or new build in Port Moody:** Ask which step code level the building is designed to. Two projects on the same street could end up built to different standards depending on when they applied. A higher step usually means better insulation and lower heating bills; a lower step may help keep costs down for the builder. Neither is automatically better for you, but it's worth knowing what you're paying for. ## My take

I've lived in Greater Vancouver for 28 years, and I've watched rules like this shift more than once. Port Moody isn't lowering its standards here; as Morrison put it, the city would "just be not accelerating it." For buyers, the practical step is simple: read the disclosure statement, ask the builder about energy specs, and don't assume every new building in the Tri-Cities, BC is built to the same level.
